Why psychology

The question I have been asking for as long as I can remember, the moment it became a purpose, and where I intend to take it next.

I

The first question

Long before I had the vocabulary for it, I was already an observer of people. Growing up in Madrid, I was fascinated by the invisible mechanics of a room: how a single arrival could shift its temperature, how two friends could hear the identical sentence and walk away with irreconcilable interpretations, how a fleeting glance could quietly rewrite someone’s decision. I wanted to understand what lay beneath the surface. Why do we react before we reflect? Why do we defend our convictions so fiercely? Why do certain moments stay with us for decades while others dissolve by evening?

Questions like these turned me into a reader. I gravitated toward anything that promised a glimpse behind behavior, and I developed a habit that has never quite left me: whenever a psychologist’s name surfaced in a book or a footnote, I would look them up and follow the trail from one thinker to the next, often far later into the night than I should have. I collected their ideas long before I could fully grasp them. Only years later, once my own studies and work gave me something to measure them against, did many of those ideas truly make sense. A few of them have since become the foundation of how I think.

Twelve years of KUMON mathematics added a second instinct that proved just as consequential: the patience to stay with a problem, alone and unhurried, until it finally yielded. Curiosity and persistence. In retrospect, they were the first two instruments of a researcher.

My education then carried me across three countries. I was raised in Madrid, continued my secondary education in England, and completed it in the United States before beginning my studies at Tulane in New Orleans. Each move was a natural experiment in perception. The same gesture could read as warmth in one place and reserve in another; the same silence could signal respect or disapproval. I learned early that behavior is unintelligible without context, and that understanding a person begins with understanding the world they are standing in.

II

A turning point

Some experiences divide a life into a before and an after. Mine arrived when I was a teenager, in the form of a loss that reorganized, almost overnight, my understanding of what matters.

I will not pretend the path forward was graceful. Grief at that age arrives without instructions, and for a long time it posed questions I could not answer. What eventually moved me was not forgetting, but a decision: to let the most difficult period of my life become the reason I would spend the rest of it in service of others.

Life is not only about surviving hard moments. It is about allowing each one to make you a better version of yourself.

What carried me through was a belief, fragile at first, that I could still be of genuine use to someone. I did not have a name for it then. Years later, in a psychology course, I would discover that it had one, and that it is among the most studied forces in human behavior.

That decision transformed a childhood fascination into a vocation. Understanding the mind ceased to be an intellectual pastime and became the most meaningful way I knew to be of service to others, in whatever form that service would take. It is also why I do not consider empathy a soft skill. For me, it is a form of knowledge, earned the hard way.

III

Curiosity becomes a discipline

At Tulane, my questions finally found a method. Psychology offered what intuition never could: a rigorous vocabulary for phenomena I had only sensed, and the empirical tools to test them rather than speculate. Names I had once looked up out of curiosity began reappearing in my coursework, and this time I had the context to understand what they had been trying to tell me. Two of them changed the way I see my own path.

The first was William James, often called the father of American psychology. I had come across his writing years earlier and been struck by one line in particular, though I admired it more than I understood it.

My experience is what I agree to attend to.

William James, The Principles of Psychology, 1890

James argued that attention is not a passive window onto the world but an act of selection. From the overwhelming abundance of sensation around us, the mind continually chooses what to take in, and in choosing, it shapes what our reality will be. What we notice becomes what we learn; what we overlook might as well not exist.

I only grasped the full weight of that idea once I joined Tulane’s Learning & Brain Development Lab. There, I help conduct experiments on how children’s attention, learning, and cognitive development unfold, and I code how they respond to different kinds of stimuli. Each coded response is, in its own way, a record of a choice a young mind has made about what deserves its attention. Watching those choices accumulate across participants, I began to see James’s point not as philosophy but as data: attention is where learning begins, and studying it closely is one of the most direct ways to understand how a person comes to know the world. A sentence I once admired from a distance is now a principle I work with every week.

The second was Albert Bandura. His early research showed that children learn not only from their own rewards and consequences but by observing the people around them, work that grew into his social cognitive theory. In 1977 he introduced the concept that would matter most to me: self-efficacy, a person’s belief in their capacity to act successfully in a given situation. Bandura demonstrated that this belief shapes which challenges we take on, how much effort we invest, and how long we persevere when things go wrong, often more than ability itself.

I recognized both ideas almost immediately. At Deep Roots, delivering individualized behavioral interventions to children, I watched modeling and reinforcement build skills one step at a time, and I watched each small success make the next attempt a little braver. That is self-efficacy in motion. I recognized it in my own story, too. The belief that I could still be useful to others, the one that had carried me forward years before, now had a name and an empirical foundation. Few things I have learned have felt more personal.

Every other setting deepened the lesson. Observing clinicians at Riverway revealed how disciplined questioning becomes a diagnosis. Supporting callers with Cruz Roja taught me how profoundly a steady voice can alter a moment of crisis. Working alongside Hispanic families at Puentes showed me how language, culture, and circumstance determine what people need. The settings varied. The subject never did: people.

William James, The Principles of Psychology, 1890.

Albert Bandura, “Self-efficacy: Toward a Unifying Theory of Behavioral Change,” Psychological Review, 1977.

IV

What I am here to do

If a single thread runs through everything I have done, it is this: I want my work to serve people. Psychology is how I understand them, and understanding is what allows me to serve them well, whether they are patients, clients, employees, or customers.

That is why I do not regard psychology as one career among many. I regard it as a lens that makes every field more human. Wherever people make decisions, seek support, or work together, behavior sits at the center, and the questions a psychologist asks can change what gets built, offered, and led.

My ambition is not modest, and I do not intend it to be. I want to bring rigorous behavioral insight into the places where it can improve the most lives, and to do it with the care that experience has taught me. I am not finished asking my questions. I suspect I never will be. But I know where I want to ask them next.

V

Where it leads

Psychology and consumer behavior

Alongside psychology, there has always been fashion. I am captivated by everything the industry encompasses: the theater of a runway show, the craft and vision behind a collection, the way a trend travels from a single atelier to city streets, the precise choreography of luxury retail. A considerable share of my free time goes to exploring it, studying how a house builds its identity season after season, how a collection is styled, cast, and photographed, how a campaign or a public relations moment can redefine how a brand is perceived. What fascinates me is never only the clothing. It is the meaning people invest in it.

So when I asked myself where my knowledge of psychology could make its most distinctive contribution, the answer felt almost inevitable. Fashion is where my discipline and my favorite pastime converge, and I am convinced that is not a coincidence. The industry is, at its core, an ongoing study of human behavior: of desire and aspiration, of belonging and self-expression, of how perception is shaped and why certain choices feel unmistakably right.

Every purchase is a compact psychological event in which perception, emotion, memory, identity, and social influence converge in the instant someone decides. Fashion intensifies all of it. What we wear is among the first signals we send the world about who we are, and one of the few choices we make about ourselves every single day.

Fittingly, that connection runs back to William James. In the same book where he wrote about attention, James described a “material self”: the parts of the outside world we come to experience as extensions of who we are. He placed the body at its core, and immediately after it, our clothing.

The body is the innermost part of the material Self in each of us… The clothes come next.

William James, The Principles of Psychology, 1890

I find it remarkable that, more than a century before branding or e-commerce, psychology already recognized that what we wear is not merely worn. It becomes part of the self, which is why choosing it can feel so personal, and why the right piece can change how someone carries themselves.

What draws me to this industry is that it already runs on psychological questions, even when it does not name them. Why does one store feel welcoming while another feels intimidating? Why does one brand earn trust while another feels hollow? Why does one garment feel unmistakably “me” when another never will? These are questions about attention, self-concept, and belonging, and they deserve to be studied with the same rigor as any laboratory phenomenon.

What I want is to explore consumer behavior with the observational discipline I practice at Tulane: to watch closely, measure carefully, and translate what people do into insight a brand can act on, so that clients are understood as people rather than transactions. Customer-facing work in Madrid and bilingual community work in New Orleans taught me that feeling genuinely understood is what makes someone return. And having lived in Spain, England, and the United States, I am especially curious about how these answers shift from one culture to the next. Psychology tells us that identity is contextual. Fashion is where that truth becomes visible.
