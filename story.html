Chapter I

The first question

Long before I knew the word for it, I was studying people. Growing up in Madrid, I watched how a room changed when someone walked in upset, how two friends could hear the same sentence and leave with opposite opinions, how a single look could change someone’s mind. I wanted to know what was happening underneath: why we react before we think, why we defend our beliefs so fiercely, why some moments stay with us for years while others are gone by dinner.

I did not have a framework for those questions yet, only the habit of asking them. Twelve years of KUMON gave me a second habit that turned out to matter just as much: the patience to stay with a problem, alone, until it made sense. Curiosity and persistence. Looking back, they were the first two instruments of a researcher.

Moving between Spain and the United States sharpened both. Every new place became a natural experiment. The same gesture could signal warmth in one country and distance in another; the same silence could mean respect or rejection. I learned early that behavior only makes sense in context, and that understanding someone’s context is the first step to understanding them.

William James defined psychology as “the science of mental life” in The Principles of Psychology (1890). I was asking his questions before I knew his name.

Chapter II

A turning point

As a teenager, I lost my closest friend. She was the most brilliant and selfless person I have ever known, and losing her changed the direction of my life.

Grief at that age does not come with instructions. For a long time I carried questions I could not answer and a weight I did not know how to put down. What eventually moved me forward was not forgetting. It was a decision: that the hardest experience of my life would become the reason I spend it helping others.

Life is not only about surviving hard moments. It is about letting each one make you a better version of yourself.

That decision turned a childhood curiosity into a calling. Understanding the mind stopped being an abstract puzzle. It became the most direct way I knew to be useful to people, especially when they need it most.

Viktor Frankl argued in Man’s Search for Meaning (1946) that people can find meaning even in suffering. I understood that idea long before I read it.

Chapter III

Curiosity becomes a discipline

At Tulane, my questions finally found a method. Psychology gave me a vocabulary for things I had only sensed, and the tools to test them instead of guessing.

Attention, which William James described as the mind taking hold of one thing out of many, is now part of my daily work. At Tulane’s Learning & Brain Development Lab, I help study how children’s attention and learning develop, one carefully coded observation at a time.

Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman showed that human judgment departs from pure logic in ways that are systematic and predictable. That idea still thrills me: even our most “irrational” choices have patterns, and patterns can be understood. Albert Bandura showed how much of what we do we learn by watching others, something I saw every day delivering behavioral interventions at Deep Roots.

Outside the lab, every role kept teaching me. Observing clinicians at Riverway showed me how careful questions become a diagnosis. Supporting callers at Cruz Roja showed me how much a calm voice can change a moment. Working with Hispanic families at Puentes showed me how language and culture shape what people need. Each setting was different. The subject was always the same: people.

Tversky and Kahneman, “Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases,” Science (1974).

Albert Bandura, Social Learning Theory (1977).

Chapter IV

What I am here to do

If there is one thread through everything I have done, it is this: I want to help people. Psychology is how I understand them, and understanding is how I can help them well.

That is why I do not see psychology as one career among many. I see it as a lens that makes every field more human. Wherever people make decisions, seek support, or work together, behavior is at the center, and a psychologist’s questions can change what gets built.

I am not finished asking those questions. I suspect I never will be. But I know where I want to ask them next.

Chapter V

Where it leads

Psychology and consumer behavior

Of everywhere psychology could take me, consumer behavior and fashion is where I want to begin. Every purchase is a small psychological event: perception, emotion, memory, identity, and social influence all meet in the moment someone decides. Clothing makes it richer still. What we wear is one of the first ways we tell the world who we are, and one of the few choices we make about ourselves every single day.

What I love about this connection is that the industry already runs on psychological questions, even when it does not call them that: why a store feels welcoming, why a brand feels trustworthy, why one piece feels like “me” and another does not. What I want is to explore that behavior with the same care for observation and data I practice in the lab, so brands can understand their clients as people rather than transactions.

And because I grew up between cultures, I am especially curious about how those answers change from Madrid to New Orleans and beyond.
